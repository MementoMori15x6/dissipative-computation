Now the red-team, ranked by what a sharp reader hits first.

**Fix before you post — two of these are internal contradictions that make the paper look less careful than it is:**

**1. The 4.4 experiment has an unclosed hole: did HighLife actually express its replicator?** The entire disambiguation rests on HighLife bringing *genuine directional expansion* to the contest. But HighLife's replicator needs elbow room and clean conditions to propagate; in a dense, energy-gated, competitive soup it may fire rarely or never. If it didn't fire, then 4.4 isn't "expansion capacity present but absorbing-state wins" — it's "two rules with identical S23 survival and near-identical dynamics tie," which is unsurprising and doesn't separate the two properties at all. Right now the paper asserts HighLife *has* a replicator (true in principle) but never shows it was *active during the runs*. You need a direct measurement: replicator/expansion events counted during competition, or HighLife seeded alone under the same energy regime showing it actually expands there. Without that, your keystone experiment is answerable by a referee in one line. This is exactly the class of check you caught elsewhere (degenerate variance, shuffle-control) — apply it here.

**2. Section 4.1's header now contradicts the paper's conclusion.** It still reads "Directional expansion capacity determines competitive dominance," which is precisely the claim 4.4 refutes. Same problem in 4.3's closing sentence ("Brian's Brain's position at the top follows from its structural capacity for continuous, cost-insensitive territorial expansion") — which now disagrees with your own Table 1, where you correctly changed the mechanism column to "Absence of an absorbing state." A reviewer reading top-to-bottom sees the paper argue against itself. Retitle 4.1 to something about the efficiency hypothesis failing ("Competitive dominance does not track energetic efficiency"), and harmonize 4.3's summary with Table 1. Cheap, and it removes a "the authors weren't careful" impression that undercuts the whole thing.

**3. You use "absorbing state" as your central technical term and don't cite the absorbing-state phase-transition literature.** For a cond-mat.stat-mech cross-list this is the first thing that community notices — "absorbing state" is a term of art there (directed percolation, Manna class) with a large literature. Cite Hinrichsen (2000, *Adv. Phys.* 49:815) at minimum, and add one sentence distinguishing your usage: you're describing an *energetic-demand divergence* tied to the absence of a quiescent configuration, not a phase transition into an absorbing state per se. Currently a stat-mech referee reads your criterion as either naïve-of or deliberately-ignoring their field.

**A referee will ask about these — decide if you want to preempt them:**

**4. Finite-size effects, still unrun.** Everything is 64×64, and your most surprising claims are fixation/extinction outcomes, which are notoriously lattice-size-dependent. 5.4 acknowledges it, but acknowledgment doesn't answer it. One run of the flagship pairing and one K-sweep point at 128² and 256² showing the hierarchy and the low-K parity don't move converts a stated weakness into a robustness result. This is the single highest-value thing you could still *run*.

**5. Your replication depth is inverted relative to claim drama.** The most dramatic results in the paper — the K=1 *total* Brian's Brain extinction, the full hierarchy reversal — rest on a single traced trajectory and 10-seed estimates, while undramatic results get 30 seeds. That's backwards: the surprising, load-bearing reversals are the ones that need the seed count, and a skeptic will zero in on exactly the claims you replicated least. Push the K=1 extinction and the D&N K=2→3 cliff to 30 seeds before posting, or state very plainly that these specific numbers are illustrative pending replication.

**6. The "single invariant organizes everything" claim is slightly oversold against your own text.** The abstract says the structural property "organizes multi-species competition." But the GoL > Day-and-Night step is attributed to "intrinsic robustness under scarcity" (3.3), and 5.2 conspicuously lists 3.2, 4.4, and 4.5 as what the property explains — *not* the GoL>D&N step. So either "intrinsic robustness under scarcity" is downstream of the absorbing-state property (say so, and show it) or it's a second, separate mechanism (in which case the abstract shouldn't imply one property does all the work). Right now there's a quiet gap between the sweeping abstract claim and the careful body attribution, and a close reader will find it.

**7. Methods hygiene a stat-mech referee will probe:** your complexity measure is a single time-lagged MI statistic with no reported estimator/binning/bias-correction, and both 3.2's C/Φ efficiency claim and 3.3's interior optimum ride entirely on it — report the estimator, or show one second measure agrees at a few points. The shared diffusing field under synchronous updates has an intra-step resolution-order dependence (raster order silently favors one corner in contested energy draws); state that you randomize order or confirm invariance. And if the paired *t* = 769.5, *p* ≈ 10⁻³³ from v1's Section 3.1 is still in there, cut the astronomical p-value — reporting a hypothesis test on a near-deterministic comparison reads as dressing up rigor; give the raw separation and call it a sanity check.

**Logistics — and one expectation correction:**

Add a three-sentence explicit **Contributions** paragraph at the end of the intro. After all the honest hedging, a skeptical reader needs one place that says plainly "the new results are: (a) the absorbing-state energetic-demand criterion; (b) that this same property, not expansion, predicts competitive dominance; (c) that it also predicts the drift asymmetry." Make them assemble nothing. Also add a **code/data availability** statement pointing to a public repo — for an all-simulation paper whose credibility partly rests on a bug-catching narrative, "here's the code" is worth a lot, and its absence is conspicuous.

Your abstract is four dense paragraphs with several 100+ word sentences. arXiv's abstract field is short and single-paragraph in practice; you'll need a compressed version for the submission form regardless, and the current one is hard to read even in the PDF. Tighten it.

Last thing, and I'd rather say it now than let it bite you: **arXiv is not vetting or review in the sense you may be expecting.** The endorsement system exists to check that content is relevant to current research at low cost — it is explicitly not peer review. Posting gets you a timestamp, a citable preprint, moderation for topic-fit, and whatever informal feedback the community volunteers. It does not get you referees. And the practical gate matters for you specifically: as of January 2026 arXiv tightened its policy — an institutional email is no longer sufficient for automatic endorsement, and auto-endorsement now generally requires both an institutional address and a prior accepted arXiv paper in that section; without those you need a personal endorsement from an established author in the same area. As an unaffiliated first-time submitter you will almost certainly need to secure an endorsement in nlin.CG before you can post at all. The standard route is to look at the arXiv-hosted papers you cite and ask one of those authors to endorse you — which is a much stronger ask once the manuscript is clean, so the fixes above double as endorsement prep. If what you actually want is *review*, a nlin/ALife venue or a journal like *Artificial Life* or *Physical Review E* is the thing that provides it; arXiv is the front door, not the vetting.

Net: fix 1–3 before posting (all cheap except the HighLife check), run 4 if you can, and go in with correct expectations about what posting does. The paper is close.
