# Displacement Competition: Is Directional Dominance Robust to a Genuinely Contested Boundary?

A systematic measurement-window adequacy audit across the rest of the project's lottery-only baselines (first calibration) and Section 3's single-rule results remains open (Section 6). Fully integrated into the manuscript as Section 4.4 (disambiguation), Section 4.6 (displacement competition, including the cost-asymmetry result), and the revised Section 5.1/5.2/5.4 (see `DC_Manuscript_Draft_v2.md` at the repo root). Terminology throughout follows the manuscript's adopted usage from spatial ecology and interacting-particle systems (Levins & Culver, 1971; Tilman, 1994; Clifford & Sudbury, 1973; Yu & Wilson, 2001): "lottery competition" for the original vacancy-only contest structure (previously called "sticky occupancy" during development), and "displacement competition" for genuine direct capture (previously "contestable occupancy").**

## 1. Motivation

Every competitive result in Sections 4.1–4.3 of the manuscript rests on a lottery-competition mechanism: an occupied cell can only be lost by first returning to the unoccupied state through its own species' internal dynamics. Neither rule can ever directly displace an occupied cell of the other species. This means Brian's Brain's dominance over Game of Life — robust to a 200-fold cost increase — may reflect which rule generates and reclaims *vacancies* fastest, not which rule wins a genuinely *contested* boundary. An established Game of Life block, once stable, is literally immune to Brian's Brain under lottery competition, since a stable block's cells never propose to change state and are therefore never eligible to be lost.

This document reports the results of adding direct displacement — an occupied cell can be taken over by the opposing species if enough of its neighbors belong to that species, in the manner of the voter model (Clifford & Sudbury, 1973) — layered on top of the existing lottery mechanism, not replacing it. It also reports a separate, later addition: a fourth pairing (Game of Life vs. HighLife) designed specifically to disambiguate whether the dominance found under lottery competition is driven by directional expansion capacity, as originally described, or by the absence of an absorbing state, as Section 3.2 independently established — a confound neither Brian's Brain pairing can resolve on its own, since Brian's Brain has both properties at once.

## 2. Method

**Mechanism**: a cell of species A becomes eligible for capture by species B if it has at least `K` neighbors of species B (Moore neighborhood). Capture takes precedence over the cell's own survival logic. This is a materially different question from Section 4's vacancy-based competition, and every design choice below was made explicit and named after two rounds of red-teaming identified several as consequential enough to silently determine the outcome if left implicit — the same failure shape as the fixed energy ceiling and fixed warmup duration caught and corrected in Section 4.2.

**Design choices, all explicit parameters, several swept rather than fixed:**
- **K (capture threshold)**: swept from 1 to 8 (the full possible range).
- **BB_PRESSURE_STATES**: which Brian's Brain states count toward capture pressure against Game of Life — swept between Firing-only and Firing+Refractory.
- **BB_MID_CYCLE_PROTECTION**: whether a Refractory cell can be captured at all — swept between True and False.
- **CAPTURED_GOL_BECOMES**: what a captured Game-of-Life cell turns into — swept between Firing (aggressive, feeds BB's expansion directly) and Refractory (the state immediately cycles toward vacancy next step, potentially self-defeating for BB). Identified in red-teaming as the single most consequential choice, not a non-question the way it is for Game of Life.
- **Energy on capture**: pinned to zero-out (not transfer) for all stages reported here.
- **Resolution order**: not applicable — the mechanism is fully vectorized and synchronous; a cell already owned by one species can only be threatened by the other species' neighbor count, never both directions at once, so there is no sequential order-dependence to introduce.

**Primary metric**: net directional capture flux (captures Game-of-Life→Brian's-Brain minus captures Brian's-Brain→Game-of-Life, tracked as a distinct event type). Aggregate territory share is reported as a secondary, confounded quantity for continuity with Section 4 only — it sums the new capture channel with the pre-existing vacancy channel and cannot on its own distinguish which is responsible for an outcome.

**Substrate**: Game of Life vs. Brian's Brain (the flagship pairing), then Day and Night vs. Brian's Brain (a second pairing, testing generalization), then Game of Life vs. Day and Night (a third pairing, with neither species a multi-state rule), then Game of Life vs. HighLife (a fourth pairing, added specifically to disambiguate expansion capacity from absorbing-state absence — see Section 3.6), all at `D_bg = 0.05`, `D_diff = 0.05` (matching Section 4.1's first tested condition).

## 3. Results

### 3.1 Mechanism validation (Stages 1–2)

Two controlled single-cell scenarios confirmed the mechanism behaves as designed before any aggregate result was trusted: a Game-of-Life cell surrounded by 8 Brian's-Brain-Firing neighbors was correctly captured (state changed, banked energy correctly zeroed), while an isolated Game-of-Life cell dying from ordinary underpopulation (no capture involved) correctly retained standard energy accounting (paying the flip cost, keeping the remainder) — confirming the capture-specific zero-out was correctly scoped and not silently applied to every transition.

### 3.2 A full sweep (K = 1–8, all parameter combinations, 10 seeds) found extreme sensitivity at low K

At `K = 7` and `K = 8`, every parameter combination converged to the same result (Game of Life holding approximately 22–25% of territory), matching Section 4.1's original sticky-occupancy result almost exactly — a clean internal consistency check confirming the new mechanism correctly reduces to the old one when captures become too rare to matter.

At `K = 1` and `K = 2`, results ranged from overwhelming Brian's Brain dominance (net capture flux exceeding +550 in Brian's Brain's favor) to a result initially reported as an aggregate territory share of exactly 1.000 for Game of Life. **This was traced directly rather than reported at face value.** A full trajectory check (`K = 1`, captured-cells-become-Refractory, Firing-only pressure) confirmed genuine, complete extinction of Brian's Brain: from an initial population of 425, a brief surge to 2,550 by step 30, followed by total collapse to zero by step 120, sustained for the remainder of the run. The mechanism: captured cells born Refractory immediately cycle toward vacancy the following step (the mandatory Refractory→OFF transition), converting every capture into self-inflicted attrition rather than held territory — Brian's Brain's own aggressive capturing becomes self-cannibalizing under this specific parameter combination, reversing the entire competitive hierarchy established in Section 4.

### 3.3 Moderate K (3–5), replicated at 30 seeds, resolves into a coherent two-factor pattern

The original 10-seed pass at K = 3–5 showed real but less extreme variation than K = 1–2. Standard deviations in this range were large relative to their means (frequently 50–100%+), indicating the original 10-seed estimates carried substantial uncertainty; this range was re-run at 30 seeds specifically to characterize it properly before drawing any conclusion.

Two consistent, mechanistically-sensible effects emerged across all three K values once properly replicated:

1. **Captured-Game-of-Life-becomes-Refractory consistently increases Game of Life's territory share relative to becomes-Firing**, at every K tested — the same self-defeating mechanism responsible for the K=1 extinction event, present in weakened form throughout this range (e.g., at K=3, no-protection: Refractory birth-state gives Game of Life a mean share of 0.519 versus 0.094 for Firing birth-state, both at Firing-only pressure).
2. **Broader Brian's Brain pressure-counting (Firing and Refractory both counting toward capture eligibility) consistently decreases Game of Life's territory share relative to Firing-only pressure**, at every K tested — mechanically easier for Brian's Brain to muster capture pressure when more of its own states contribute to it.

Both effects shrink in magnitude from the extreme K=1–2 range through K=3–5, and vanish entirely by K=7–8, where captures become too rare for any of these definitional choices to matter. No combination in the K=3–5 range showed Game of Life's territory share exceeding roughly 0.52 on average, and most combinations remained well below 0.5 — Brian's Brain generally retains a territory advantage in this range, but a substantially reduced one relative to the original sticky-occupancy baseline (~0.23), except in the specific combination most favorable to Game of Life noted above.

### 3.4 A second pairing (Day and Night vs. Brian's Brain) confirms the sensitivity generalizes, but in a qualitatively different shape

Day and Night was tested against Brian's Brain under the identical mechanism, to test whether the design-sensitivity found for Game of Life is general or specific to that one pairing. At `K = 3` (the same value used for initial mechanism validation), Day and Night went **fully extinct in every seed tested (8 of 8)**, mostly through the pre-existing vacancy channel alone — several seeds showed zero captures occurring in either direction before Day and Night's population collapsed entirely, consistent with its independently-established fragility under this substrate (Sections 3.3, 4.3 of the manuscript).

A sweep of `K = 1–3` across all parameter combinations (10 seeds each) found that low K reproduces the same self-defeating-capture escape valve found for Game of Life, more dramatically. **This was subsequently replicated at 30 seeds for the specific combination (Refractory birth-state, Firing-only pressure) driving the effect** — using the standard measurement window (200-step warmup, 100-step window) used throughout this project. At `K = 1`, the escape valve is fully reliable: Brian's Brain went completely extinct in all 30 of 30 trials. At `K = 2`, this initial pass found Brian's Brain extinct in 22 of 30 trials — a strong majority but, it seemed, not a deterministic outcome. At `K = 3`, the outcome appeared to reverse: Day and Night extinct in 25 of 30 trials. Extended to `K = 4–8`, the same pattern continued: 23 of 30 at `K=4`, 13 of 30 at `K=5`, reaching a plateau of 8 of 30 at `K=6–8` matching the original lottery-competition baseline.

**This entire picture for K=2 through K=5 turned out to be a measurement artifact, not a real finding, and the investigation that caught it is reported here in full rather than folded quietly into corrected numbers.** Checking whether `K=2`'s apparent non-determinism (22 of 30) reflected a genuine second stable outcome or an unresolved transient — the natural next question, and the reason this check was done at all — found that every one of the 8 "surviving" trials at the standard 100-step window was still in the same slow decay toward Brian's Brain's extinction as the other 22, simply not yet finished. Extending the window to 3,000 steps (verified unchanged at 5,000 and 10,000, ruling out this being merely "still not long enough") showed every one of those 8 trials reach zero. The true, equilibrated result at `K=2` is fully deterministic: 30 of 30, identical to `K=1`. The same check applied to `K=3`'s apparent reversal (25 of 30) found the identical problem in the opposite direction — the 5 "surviving" Day-and-Night trials were also still decaying — with a true equilibrated result of 30 of 30. `K=4` corrects the same way (23 of 30 to 30 of 30). `K=5` corrects partially (13 of 30 to 27 of 30 — most, but not all, of the apparent partial escape was transient). `K=6` through `K=8`, by contrast, were checked against the same possibility and found genuine: population counts at the standard window were identical, cell for cell, to a 3,000-step window, confirming the reported plateau was never an artifact.

The corrected picture is a sharp, fully deterministic reversal between `K=2` and `K=3` — Day and Night wins completely at `K ≤ 2`, Brian's Brain wins completely at `K = 3–4` — followed by a separate, genuinely gradual relaxation from that peak down to the `K=6` plateau (`K=5`'s 27 of 30 sits between the two). This is a materially different, sharper shape than "a smooth, gradual, two-factor sensitivity" — the gradualness that remains is real, but it lives entirely in the K=4-to-6 relaxation, not in the K=2-to-3 transition, which is a sharp step once properly measured. The likely explanation for the reversal itself is unchanged: Day and Night's already-established intrinsic fragility under energetic scarcity gives it a narrow window (`K ≤ 2`) in which the self-defeating-capture mechanism can rescue it from Brian's Brain, closing sharply once capture becomes even slightly harder to muster.

**This finding is reported with a deliberately narrow scope.** It was found by chance, in the course of investigating one specific apparent non-determinism for one specific pairing. Whether the standard 100-step window is adequate for the other three pairings in this document, or for the lottery-only baselines elsewhere in this project, has not been checked and should not be assumed either way — see Section 6, next steps, for this as a new, separate, and higher-priority item than the others in this list.

### 3.5 A third pairing (Game of Life vs. Day and Night, no Brian's Brain) shows the cleanest result of the three

Both prior pairings involved Brian's Brain, whose multi-state structure introduces the birth-state and pressure-definition ambiguities responsible for much of the sensitivity found in Sections 3.2–3.4. This third pairing removes that confound entirely: Game of Life and Day and Night are both ordinary, single-occupied-state Life-like rules, so a captured cell simply becomes the capturing species with no other option, and pressure-counting has no ambiguity. The only free parameter is K itself.

A sweep of `K = 1–8` (10 seeds, then K = 1–3 replicated at 30 seeds given how consequential the result looked) found a sharp, three-part transition, confirmed to be a real, low-variance effect rather than noise (standard deviations of 0.007–0.024 at the properly replicated points, far tighter than either Brian's-Brain pairing's moderate-K range):

- **K = 1–2: near-perfect territorial parity** (mean Game-of-Life share 0.516 ± 0.024 at K=1, 0.499 ± 0.007 at K=2), with **zero extinctions on either side** — a dramatic departure from the original sticky-occupancy result (Section 4.3), where Game of Life crushed Day and Night 99.49% to 0.51%.
- **K = 3: a sharp reversal to near-total Game of Life dominance** (0.991 ± 0.012), with Day and Night going extinct in 18 of 30 trials — briefly exceeding even the original sticky-occupancy baseline.
- **K = 6–8: reconvergence to the original sticky-occupancy result** (~0.996, matching 99.49% closely) as captures become too rare to matter, the same consistency check found for the other two pairings.

This is, we think, the cleanest and most direct confirmation across all three original pairings that boundary-contestation sensitivity is a property of displacement competition itself, not merely an artifact of Brian's Brain's particular structure. A pairing that showed one of the most decisive blowouts anywhere in the manuscript (Section 4.3) collapses to a genuine coin-flip purely by changing how easy direct displacement is, with no multi-state ambiguity available to attribute the swing to. The most plausible mechanism: at very low K, both species can displace each other with roughly equal ease, and with no structural asymmetry between them to break the tie, the outcome comes to reflect the underlying symmetry of the displacement mechanism itself rather than either species' real competitive advantage under the original lottery contest — suggesting that a meaningful share of Game of Life's original dominance over Day and Night may have reflected which species holds and reclaims vacant territory more efficiently under a lottery regime, not which one would win a genuinely, symmetrically contested boundary.

### 3.6 A fourth pairing (Game of Life vs. HighLife) disambiguates expansion capacity from absorbing-state absence

Every pairing reported above involving Brian's Brain confounds two properties that Section 3.2 and Sections 4.1–4.3 had, up to this point, offered as separate explanations for the same phenomenon: Brian's Brain lacks an absorbing state, *and* it possesses genuine directional expansion capacity (mandatory ignition into new territory). Because Brian's Brain has both properties simultaneously, none of the results above can distinguish which one is actually responsible for its dominance.

HighLife (rule B36/S23, devised by Nathan Thompson in 1994) breaks this confound directly. It shares Game of Life's exact survival condition (S23), so it retains every one of Game of Life's absorbing states — identical still-lifes, oscillators, and gliders. Its additional birth condition (B6), however, supports a well-documented, genuine self-replicator: a small pattern that copies itself indefinitely along a diagonal line — directional expansion capacity that plain Game of Life structurally lacks. If expansion capacity alone drove Brian's Brain's dominance in Sections 4.1–4.3, HighLife should dominate Game of Life similarly. If absorbing-state absence is the operative property, HighLife's expansion capacity should not be sufficient, since HighLife can still settle into quiescence just as Game of Life can.

**Under lottery competition** (the mechanism used in Sections 4.1–4.3), Game of Life and HighLife were found to be roughly evenly matched: mean Game-of-Life territory share 0.549 versus 0.451 for HighLife across 15 seeds, with zero extinctions on either side — nothing resembling Brian's Brain's 94.7–99.65% dominance over every other rule tested.

**Under displacement competition**, the same rough parity held across the entire tested range of the capture threshold `K = 1` through `8`: Game of Life's share stayed bounded between 0.486 and 0.597 throughout, with zero extinctions and no sharp transitions of the kind found in every pairing involving Brian's Brain or Day and Night. Unlike those pairings, no dramatic K-dependent swing, self-defeating-displacement escape valve, or extinction event appeared anywhere in the range tested.

This resolves the confound in favor of the absorbing-state property. HighLife's genuine expansion capacity does not translate into Brian's-Brain-style dominance under either contest structure, because HighLife — sharing Game of Life's absorbing states — can also settle into quiescence, and its capacity to expand while active does not compound into a decisive, sustained advantage the way Brian's Brain's inability to ever settle does. The dramatic sensitivity to displacement-mechanism design found throughout Sections 3.2–3.5 appears to be specific to pairings in which at least one competitor lacks an absorbing state, not a general property of directional expansion capacity as such.

### 3.7 Cost asymmetry combined with displacement competition: dominance strengthens rather than merely surviving

Section 4.2 of the manuscript established that Brian's Brain's dominance under lottery competition survives a two-hundred-fold increase in its own metabolic cost. Whether this holds once displacement is also in play was untested until this section: the cost-multiplier convention from Section 4.2 was extended to displacement competition, charging the multiplier for any transition involving a Brian's Brain state, including displacement in either direction (a Game-of-Life cell being captured by Brian's Brain, or a Brian's Brain cell being captured by Game of Life).

The flagship pairing was tested at four capture thresholds (`K = 1, 3, 5, 8`) across three cost multipliers (`α = 1, 20, 200`), 10 seeds each, with the same two corrections required in Section 4.2's original sweep applied again here (ceiling and warmup duration both scaled with α, verified against the α=1 baseline before trusting higher values):

| K | α=1 | α=20 | α=200 |
|---|---|---|---|
| 1 | 0.566 ± 0.008 | 0.538 ± 0.023 | 0.602 ± 0.074 |
| 3 | 0.084 ± 0.078 | 0.195 ± 0.150 | 0.098 ± 0.159 |
| 5 | 0.205 ± 0.181 | 0.075 ± 0.055 | 0.033 ± 0.007 |
| 8 | 0.232 ± 0.168 | 0.085 ± 0.057 | 0.036 ± 0.008 |

(Figures are mean Game-of-Life territory share ± standard deviation.)

At `K = 1`, cost has essentially no effect — Game of Life's share stays within a narrow band regardless of α, consistent with this threshold's near-parity regime found in Sections 3.2 and 3.6. At `K = 5` and `K = 8`, Brian's Brain's dominance does not merely survive the cost increase — it strengthens substantially, and the seed-to-seed variance collapses by roughly an order of magnitude over the same range, indicating the high-cost regime is more deterministically favorable to Brian's Brain, not merely equally favorable. `K = 3` shows no clear monotonic trend and the highest variance at every cost level tested, consistent with this threshold sitting near the sensitivity transition already identified in Section 3.2.

Cost-insensitivity is therefore not an artifact specific to the lottery contest structure in which it was originally found (Section 4.2): it extends to displacement competition at the thresholds where Brian's Brain's advantage is largest, strengthening the case that this is a robust, structural property of the absorbing-state distinction rather than a coincidental result of one particular contest mechanism.

### 3.8 A second background-inflow calibration: initially reported, then found to need the same correction as Section 3.4 -- corrected in full

Every result in Sections 3.1-3.7 was obtained at one background-inflow/diffusion condition: `D_bg = 0.05`, `D_diff = 0.05`, described throughout this project as Brian's Brain's own calibrated threshold. The manuscript's Section 4.1 also tested a second condition -- background inflow calibrated to Game of Life's own, much lower threshold (`D_bg = 0.01`, `D_diff = 0.03`, the same value used elsewhere in this project, e.g. `03-diffusion-limited-scarcity/stage1_gause_neutral_competition.py`) -- under which Brian's Brain's lottery-competition dominance was, if anything, more extreme (94.7-96.0% versus 55.6-96.7% at the first condition).

**Initial pass (superseded below).** All four displacement pairings were first re-run at this second condition using the standard measurement window (200-step warmup, 100-step window, used throughout this project), at `K = 1, 3, 5, 8`, 15 seeds each. This pass reported the same qualitative pattern as the first condition, with a speed-of-reconvergence difference at two of three sensitive pairings.

**That pass was then checked directly against a substantially longer window, and found inadequate.** This check was prompted by Section 3.4's finding, above, that the standard window can mistake a transient for a steady state near a sharp transition. Comparing the standard-window results to a 5,000-step window (confirmed sufficient -- no further change at 15,000) found **13 of 16 pairing/K combinations tested had moved**, most substantially. The standard-window numbers for this condition were, for most of the sweep, transients rather than steady states -- the same failure mode as Section 3.4, now confirmed to generalize beyond one pairing.

**Corrected results, full K=1-8 range, confirmed-adequate window (10 seeds per point, escalated to 30 where variance warranted it):**

|---|---|---|---|---|
| 1 | 0.459 | 0.501 | 1.000 | 0.497 |
| 2 | 0.500 | 0.500 | 1.000 | 0.499 |
| 3 | 0.000 | 1.000 | 0.000 | 0.233 |

Three of the four pairings now show a **sharp, fully deterministic transition between K=2 and K=3** -- not the smoother pattern the standard-window pass reported. Game of Life vs. Brian's Brain and Day and Night vs. Brian's Brain both flip completely at this boundary, then relax gradually back toward their lottery baseline by K=6-8. Game of Life vs. Day and Night flips completely and reconverges by K=6, matching its directly-measured lottery baseline (0.993) almost exactly, as does Day and Night vs. Brian's Brain (0.0023, an exact match).

**Game of Life vs. HighLife shows no sharp transition, as at the first condition -- but a different, previously unknown property surfaced in its place.** Its near-parity outcome is markedly high-variance at this calibration (sd 0.24-0.27 from K=3 onward, 30-seed replication), unlike the low-variance stability found at the first condition (sd 0.14-0.18). This was confirmed as a genuine, calibration-dependent property, not a K-sweep artifact: the lottery-only baseline, independently re-measured at the same adequate window, shows the identical high variance (0.527, sd 0.256), and individual seed outcomes at several displacement K-values matched the lottery baseline's seed-by-seed values closely (e.g. K=8's 10-seed values line up with the lottery baseline's to within sampling noise, seed for seed). K=1-2 are the exception, staying low-variance (sd 0.007-0.015), consistent with capture events at this threshold dominating the outcome regardless of the underlying high-variance dynamics elsewhere in the sweep.

**The K=3 dip was investigated directly, and resolved.** K=3's mean (0.233) sits noticeably below every other K from 4 through 8 (which cluster around 0.50-0.53), a gap too large for sampling noise alone (standard error ≈ 0.048 at n=30). Inspecting the full 30-seed distribution at K=3 found it is not merely lower on average but qualitatively different in shape from K=4's: K=3 has 5 of 30 seeds showing complete Game-of-Life extinction (territory share exactly 0), and a further dozen seeds clustered below 0.2, while K=4's 30-seed distribution has no extinctions at all and spreads roughly uniformly from 0.08 to 1.0. Checking every adjacent K confirmed this is a narrow, isolated spike, not part of a broader low-K trend: Game-of-Life extinction occurred in 0 of 10 seeds at K=1, 0 of 20 at K=2, 5 of 30 at K=3, 0 of 30 at K=4, and 0 of 10 at K=5.

The most likely mechanism: `K=3` is the one capture threshold that exactly coincides with both species' ordinary birth-neighbor condition (Game of Life's B3, and the B3 half of HighLife's B36 rule). At every other K tested, the capture threshold sits away from this value, so capture eligibility and birth eligibility are largely decoupled; at K=3 specifically, a cell's local neighbor count can simultaneously satisfy the capture threshold for one species and the birth condition for the other, creating a resonance between the two mechanisms that plausibly makes an early, self-reinforcing Game-of-Life collapse more likely in a genuine minority of trials, without affecting the mean outcome in the majority. This is offered as the most likely explanation given the evidence (a clean, narrow spike exactly at the shared birth-neighbor value, absent one step in either direction), not as a fully isolated causal proof; confirming it directly would require testing a variant where the birth and capture neighbor-count conventions are decoupled, which has not been done.

This finding was not sought independently -- it was found in the course of correcting the equilibration problem, exactly the same way Section 3.4's correction surfaced a sharper transition than originally reported. Both findings share the same lesson: near a sharp transition or a high-variance regime, the standard 100-step window cannot be trusted without a direct check against a much longer one.

### 3.9 Cost asymmetry extended to Day and Night vs. Brian's Brain: the flagship pattern holds at moderate-to-high K, but reverses sharply at K=1

Section 3.7 tested cost asymmetry under displacement for the flagship pairing only. The same test (Brian's Brain's transitions priced at `α × FLIP_COST`, ceiling and warmup both scaled with α, verified against α=1 before trusting higher values) was extended to Day and Night vs. Brian's Brain, using the combination already established for this pairing (Section 3.4): captured Day-and-Night cell becomes BB_REFRACTORY, `BB_PRESSURE_STATES = {BB_FIRING}`.

At `K = 5` and `K = 8`, the flagship pattern held closely: Brian's Brain's dominance was essentially unaffected by cost, with Day-and-Night's territory share staying in a narrow band (0.001–0.004) across `α = 1` to `200`.

At `K = 1`, the result is the opposite of the flagship pattern, and the sharpest cost effect found anywhere in this project. At `α = 1`, the self-defeating-displacement escape (Section 3.4) is complete and fully deterministic: Day and Night wins all territory in 10 of 10 seeds. Any `α ≥ 2` flips this completely and deterministically: Brian's Brain wins all territory in 10 of 10 seeds, with Day and Night going fully extinct every time. A finer sweep confirmed this is a genuine, resolved transition rather than a discontinuity, not an artifact of coarse sampling: `α = 1.3` still gives full Day-and-Night dominance, `α = 1.5` shows slight degradation (0.983 mean share), `α = 1.7` is mostly flipped (0.193 mean, high variance), and `α = 2.0` onward is fully flipped. This was checked against a windowing artifact directly — measurement window varied from 100 to 3,000 steps at `α = 20`, with the result unchanged throughout, ruling out the "recharge time exceeds window length" failure mode that this same fix pattern (scaling warmup with `α/D_bg`) exists to prevent.

**Mechanism**: the K=1 escape valve's self-defeating property depends on a captured Day-and-Night cell's mandatory Refractory-to-OFF transition actually completing promptly. That transition is a Brian's-Brain-state transition, and is therefore priced under this section's convention exactly like every other Brian's Brain transition. Once it becomes even marginally more expensive than the surrounding dynamics can sustain, the escape valve itself closes — captured cells linger in Refractory state for longer, Brian's Brain's territorial footprint grows instead of self-cannibalizing, and the entire competitive hierarchy at this threshold reverses. This is a genuinely different phenomenon from Sections 4.2/3.7's finding that pricing Brian's Brain's *ordinary* dominance does not erode it — here, pricing eliminates a *reversal* of that dominance instead, and does so far more sharply than any other cost effect found in this project.

### 3.10 Cost asymmetry extended to Game of Life vs. HighLife: no shift in outcome, but a shift from stable coexistence to bimodal winner-take-all

HighLife has no "entire existence" analogous to Brian's Brain's to price — it shares Game of Life's ordinary, cheap B3/S23 rule, and only its extra B6 birth condition (the genuine self-replicator disambiguated in Section 3.6) is a distinct "extra machinery" to test for cost sensitivity. The natural, surgical cost convention charges `α × FLIP_COST` only to births triggered specifically by the B6 condition, leaving ordinary B3 births, S23 survival, and both capture directions at base cost.

At `K = 1, 3, 8` (representative points spanning this pairing's already-established near-parity range), the mean outcome barely moved at any cost level: Game of Life's share stayed within roughly 0.40–0.57 throughout, consistent with Section 3.6's finding that this pairing does not produce decisive dominance in either direction. Variance, however, rose sharply with cost at `K = 3` and `K = 8` — standard deviation grew from 0.16–0.17 at `α = 1` to 0.33–0.46 at `α = 200` — and individual seed outcomes at high cost became visibly bimodal rather than centered on the mean: most runs at `α = 200`, `K = 8` landed near-total dominance for one side or the other (e.g. 0.99, 0.98, 0.99 alongside 0.016, 0.008, 0.017 in the same 10-seed batch), rarely near 0.5. `K = 1` was an exception, remaining stable and low-variance (0.50–0.51) at every cost level tested, consistent with capture events at this threshold being frequent enough to dominate the outcome regardless of the B6 mechanism's price.

Pricing HighLife's self-replicator, in other words, does not shift which side tends to win on average — but it destabilizes what was previously a comparatively stable coexistence into a winner-take-all dynamic, where a small early advantage compounds rather than resolving into a sustained near-parity split. This is a third, qualitatively distinct pattern of cost sensitivity in this project, different from both the flagship pairing's insensitivity and Day and Night vs. Brian's Brain's sharp K=1 reversal.

### 3.11 Precedence rule sweep: capture-always-wins vs. coin-flip, tested and found not to matter

Every result reported above pins one precedence rule: capture always overrides a cell's own survival logic. This only matters for Game of Life cells (Brian's Brain has no survival option at all -- both its states transition unconditionally). A Game-of-Life cell can be both capture-eligible (>= K Brian's-Brain-pressure neighbors) and would-otherwise-survive (2 or 3 GOL neighbors) simultaneously; the pinned rule lets capture win unconditionally whenever both apply.

The alternative proposed early in this project -- a coin flip whenever both conditions apply, rather than capture winning automatically -- was tested on the flagship pairing at `K = 1, 3, 5, 8` (15 seeds each). `K = 1` and `K = 3` showed no visible difference (0.534 vs. 0.535, and 0.051 vs. 0.050, capture-wins vs. coinflip respectively). `K = 5` and `K = 8` showed an apparent gap at 15 seeds (0.203 vs. 0.315 at K=5; 0.237 vs. 0.260 at K=8) large enough to warrant the project's standing rule against trusting a small-n dramatic difference. Both were re-run at 30 seeds: the K=5 gap shrank (0.211 vs. 0.277) and was not statistically distinguishable from noise (two-sample t-test, p = 0.18); K=8 showed no difference at all (p = 0.92).

**This precedence choice does not measurably affect the flagship pairing's competitive outcome**, at any K tested, once properly replicated. This stands in contrast to the capture birth-state and pressure-definition choices characterized in Sections 3.2-3.4, which are demonstrably consequential -- not every design choice in this mechanism turns out to matter, and this one was checked rather than assumed.

## 4. Interpretation

Brian's Brain's dominance under lottery competition is **not robust** to the introduction of genuine displacement, and this now holds across all three original pairings tested, not just one. Whether it survives, and how decisively, depends critically on implementation choices for which there is no single canonical answer — most starkly demonstrated by the K=1 extinction events (in both Brian's-Brain pairings), where one "reasonable" choice of what a displaced cell becomes reverses the entire competitive hierarchy from total dominance to total extinction. This is itself a real finding: the manuscript's Section 4 dominance hierarchy should not be read as a general property of "expansion capacity versus efficiency" holding under any contest structure, but specifically as a property of a lottery regime that rewards continuous vacancy-generation — a thermodynamically-gated instance of a distinction long established in spatial ecology (Levins & Culver, 1971; Tilman, 1994; Yu & Wilson, 2001), exactly as the manuscript's Section 5.1 now states directly rather than leaves as a theoretical concern.

At the same time, the two-factor pattern found in Game of Life's moderate-K range (Section 3.3) shows this sensitivity is not arbitrary noise — it follows directly and predictably from the mechanism's own logic (self-defeating displacement under one birth-state choice; easier displacement-pressure accumulation under a broader pressure definition), and both effects behave consistently across the entire range tested. The second pairing (Section 3.4) adds an important refinement: the same underlying sensitivity manifests in a qualitatively different shape depending on the competitor's own intrinsic robustness — a smooth, mechanistically-legible gradient across a wide K range for a robust species (Game of Life), versus a sharp, narrow-window cliff for an intrinsically fragile one (Day and Night). The third pairing (Section 3.5) is the most conclusive of the three originals, because it removes Brian's Brain's multi-state structure entirely and still finds a dramatic, low-variance, confirmed swing (from a 99.5%-to-0.5% blowout down to a genuine coin-flip) purely as a function of displacement difficulty — ruling out the possibility that boundary-contestation sensitivity is specific to Brian's Brain's particular rule structure.

**The fourth pairing (Section 3.6) resolves a confound the other three could not.** Because it separates directional expansion capacity from absorbing-state absence — properties Brian's Brain has simultaneously — and finds that expansion capacity alone (HighLife) produces neither dramatic dominance nor displacement-sensitivity, while absorbing-state absence (Brian's Brain) produces both, this document's central conclusion sharpens from "the sensitivity generalizes across rule pairings" to a more specific structural claim: the sensitivity is tied to whether a competitor admits an absorbing state, not to expansion capacity as such. This is now the manuscript's Section 5.2 unifying claim, and this document's own results are what directly support it.

## 5. What this does NOT establish

- **Four pairings were initially tested at only one background inflow / diffusion condition** (`D_bg = 0.05`, `D_diff = 0.05`, matching Section 4.1's first tested condition). **This has since been extended**: all four pairings were re-run at Section 4.1's second condition (`D_bg = 0.01`, `D_diff = 0.03`, Game of Life's own calibrated threshold), with lottery baselines measured directly rather than assumed (Section 3.8). The same qualitative K-sensitivity pattern held at every pairing. This second-condition test is coarser than the first, however — 15 seeds and four K-values rather than 30 seeds and a full sweep — and surfaced a speed-of-reconvergence difference across conditions that is noted but not yet resolved.
- **Day and Night's K-sweep (against Brian's Brain) has now been replicated at 30 seeds across the full K=1–8 range, and this surfaced a measurement-window problem, not just a sampling one**: an initial 10-seed pass reported K=2 as showing universal Brian's Brain extinction; the first 30-seed replication (at the standard 100-step window) found only 22 of 30. Investigating that apparent non-determinism directly found it was a transient, not a real second outcome — extending the window to 3,000+ steps showed the true result is fully deterministic (30 of 30), and the same problem, corrected the same way, affected K=3 (25→30 of 30), K=4 (23→30 of 30), and K=5 (13→27 of 30). K=6–8 were checked against the same possibility and confirmed genuine (identical population counts at 100 vs. 3,000 steps). The corrected picture is a sharp, deterministic reversal between K=2 and K=3, not the gradual transition originally reported. All corrected in Section 3.4 above. **This was found by chance while investigating one specific pairing; whether the same window-adequacy problem affects any other result in this project (the other three displacement pairings, or the lottery-only baselines) has not been checked.**
- **The K=1 extinction results for both Brian's-Brain pairings have now been replicated at 30 seeds, confirmed deterministic**: Game of Life against Brian's Brain shows extinction in 30 of 30 trials (standard deviation exactly zero); Day and Night against Brian's Brain likewise shows 30 of 30. This closes the evidentiary gap relative to the moderate-K and third/fourth-pairing work.
- **The HighLife disambiguation (Section 3.6) rests on one pairing and establishes that expansion capacity alone is not sufficient for Brian's-Brain-style dominance — it does not establish that expansion capacity contributes nothing to competitive outcomes in general**, only that absorbing-state absence is the operative property in the cases tested here. A rule with genuine expansion capacity but a harder-to-reach absorbing state than Game of Life's remains untested.
- **Precedence (displacement always overriding the victim's own survival rule) has now been tested against a coin-flip-resolved alternative** (Section 3.11), for the flagship pairing at K=1,3,5,8, with the two points showing any visible gap (K=5, K=8) replicated at 30 seeds specifically to check against a small-n overstatement. Neither point held up as a real difference (p=0.18 and p=0.92 respectively) — this precedence choice does not appear to matter for this pairing's outcome, unlike the capture birth-state and pressure-definition choices, which demonstrably do.
- **Cost asymmetry combined with displacement competition has now been tested for all three pairings where a natural cost convention applies** (Sections 3.7, 3.9, 3.10). The flagship pairing and Day and Night vs. Brian's Brain's moderate-to-high thresholds confirm the original cost-insensitivity finding (dominance strengthens with cost). Day and Night vs. Brian's Brain's lowest threshold (K=1) shows the opposite: a sharp, mechanistically-explained reversal once Brian's Brain's mandatory cycling is priced above roughly 1.5-2x baseline. Game of Life vs. HighLife (priced only on its B6 mechanism) shows a third pattern: stable mean outcome, but variance that grows sharply and turns bimodal at high cost. None of this was anticipated by the flagship pairing's result alone — cost sensitivity under displacement is pairing-specific and mechanism-specific, not a single generalizable finding.
- **This document's findings are now fully integrated into the manuscript** (Sections 4.4, 4.6, 5.1, 5.2, 5.4).
